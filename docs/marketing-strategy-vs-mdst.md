# Marketing strategy — outrunning MDST Market

**Date:** 2026-08-13 · **Status:** active
**Source:** full competitive intel pass on MDST Market, Slate, Kauna, Modanisa, Modest Path,
From The Labels — see the research summary in this session's transcript (not yet a docs/log
entry; this file supersedes a chat-only writeup and is the one to keep current).

This file has two parts: (1) what actually works in MDST's own content, verified from their
real posts, and why; (2) the marketing strategy for The Modesty House built from that — plus
a reusable prompt at the bottom to re-run this research and refresh the doc later.

---

## 1. What works in MDST's content, and why (verified from their real posts)

All figures below are from a direct fetch of MDST's Instagram (@mdst_market, public
`web_profile_info` API), TikTok (@the.mdst, public data blob), Pinterest (mdstmarket),
and Substack (mdstmarket.substack.com public archive API), on 2026-08-13.

### The pattern, ranked by how consistently it wins

1. **Real people beat brand voice, every time, on every platform.**
   - TikTok: their #1 video ever (1,566 views) is a creator collab tagging an influencer.
     Their #2 (836 views) is a founder/team "behind the scenes" piece. Everything else —
     87 videos of brand-voice messaging — flatlines in a 123–179 view band. That's a
     ~5–10x gap between "a person talking" and "the brand talking."
   - Instagram: same shape. Their literal value-proposition post ("modest shopping
     shouldn't feel like a full-time job") is their **worst-performing post on both
     platforms simultaneously**. Nobody engages with the brand asserting its own pitch.

2. **UGC / community features are the only reliable comment driver.**
   - The two Instagram posts with the highest comment counts (18 and 14, against a median
     of 2) are both "community favourites" posts featuring followers' own outfits, actively
     solicited ("Send us a photo of yourself in your cultural attire — DM to be featured").
   - This works because it's reciprocal: the featured person shares it, their followers see
     it, and the brand did none of the creative work.

3. **Cultural/seasonal moments outperform everyday content by a wide margin.**
   - Their single best Instagram post ever, by every metric, is a Ramadan reel (126 likes,
     1,602 views, 1.41% engagement rate — 2–3x their normal rate). It's pinned.
   - The mechanism: seasonal moments carry pre-existing search and social intent that the
     brand doesn't have to manufacture.

4. **Platform-native humor beats on-brand messaging.**
   - A meme post (Spider-Man format) outperformed every product post that week (77 likes).
     It cost nothing to make and doesn't mention the product at all.

5. **The right structural idea, abandoned, is worse than never having it.**
   - Pinterest: 109 pins across 11 well-designed **occasion-based boards** (Wedding Guest,
     Graduation, Coffee Date, Game Day, Streetwear) — exactly the taxonomy that maps to
     high-intent shopping search on that platform. 3 followers, dormant since 2026-07-24.
   - Substack: created 8 months ago, cross-promoted on every page of the site and every
     social bio, 21 subscribers, **zero posts ever published**.
   - Lesson for us, not just a jab at them: starting a channel is a liability, not an
     asset, until there's a real cadence behind it. Better to run fewer channels well.

### What this tells us about their audience (not just their content)

The people who *do* engage with MDST respond to: being seen as individuals (UGC features),
seeing the humans who built it (founder content), and moments tied to real occasions in
their life (Ramadan, weddings, graduation — Pinterest's board list is basically a wishlist
of "moments modest shoppers dress for"). They do **not** respond to being told, by a brand,
what the brand's value proposition is. That's a generalizable finding about this audience,
not just a critique of MDST's execution.

## 2. The Modesty House marketing strategy

Built directly against the above, plus the structural facts from the fuller competitive
report: MDST's catalogue is aggregated (Temu/AliExpress/boohoo alongside indie labels,
self-contradictory size claims), their AI filtering demonstrably leaks (a reviewer's set
neckline preference wasn't honored — the same failure independently shows up at Slate and
Kauna), they're US-only in practice, and they have far more supply-side apparatus (14 staff,
$599/mo brand tiers) than actual users (8 iOS ratings, 217 Android installs).

### Content pillars, in priority order

**1. Show the curation happening — not the catalogue, the judgment behind it.**
This is the single hardest thing for a faceless AI-search company to fake, and it's the
exact inverse of their weak spot. Concrete formats:
- "Why this one, not that one" — two visually similar pieces from different brands, one
  editorial reason it made the cut and one didn't. Doesn't require a face on camera.
- A running "what we cut this week and why" post, tied to real exclusions.json entries
  (never disclose brand names being blocklisted for legal/relationship reasons — but
  categories of cuts, e.g. "we don't carry mass-market synthetic abayas," are fair game).
- This directly weaponizes the category-wide AI-leak finding: "an algorithm can miss a
  neckline preference. A person can't miss it twice."

**2. Real person on camera, consistently — this is the highest-leverage single change.**
MDST's data proves this isn't optional, it's the entire game: a founder/team piece beat
their brand-voice baseline by 5–7x, and a single creator collab was their best post ever,
done once and never repeated (their gap, our opportunity). If Tina (or whoever curates) is
willing to be on camera even occasionally — explaining a pick, doing a "3 things I rejected
this week," reacting to a reader-submitted outfit — that alone likely outperforms anything
else in this document. If not, borrowed faces work too: brand founders, or readers.

**3. UGC / reader features, run as a standing format, not a one-off.**
Same mechanic MDST proved works (14–18 comments vs a median of 2): solicit readers' own
modest outfits built from linked brands, feature them weekly. Low cost, high reciprocity,
and it turns readers into distribution — each featured person shares it to their own
audience, which MDST's own funnel data shows is worth more than paid reach (their Reels get
1.9–6.7% of their own follower base; a shared feature reaches an audience that was never
following the brand at all).

**4. Take Pinterest, seriously, now.** MDST proved the taxonomy (occasion-based boards) is
correct and then abandoned it at 3 followers. This is close to a walk-in win: build the same
occasion-based board structure (wedding guest, interview, graduation, Eid, wedding-guest
modest, summer modest, hijab-friendly workwear) using your own catalogue, pin consistently.
High-intent shopping search traffic, essentially uncontested in this category right now.

**5. Own long-form/editorial content they structurally can't sustain.** Their Substack has
zero posts in 8 months despite being promoted everywhere — a 13-person VC-narrative startup
apparently can't keep a newsletter running. A curated editorial voice is exactly what a
taste-led site should be able to sustain that an AI-search company can't manufacture:
styling guides tied to real occasions, brand spotlights explaining *why* a brand earned a
place in the directory, seasonal capsule pieces. This doubles as the SEO surface MDST has
completely ceded (see the competitive report's differentiation-plan §3).

**6. Cultural/seasonal calendar, planned ahead, not reactive.** Ramadan was MDST's best post
ever by a wide margin, and it's an annual event they'll always be able to do too — but
weddings, Eid, back-to-school modest workwear, and (since this is Europe-based) local
cultural/seasonal moments across the markets you actually serve, are all available and
under-used by everyone in this space. Build a content calendar around real occasions, not
generic "new arrivals" posts.

**7. Cross-community framing, but earned rather than stated.** MDST's "modesty has never
had just one definition" line performs well for them, but it's a tagline pasted onto a
generic product feed. If The Modesty House's catalogue genuinely spans multiple aesthetics
and communities, the credible version of this message is showing it — a hijabi reader's
outfit next to a modest-but-secular reader's outfit next to a modest Christian reader's
outfit, styled from the same catalogue — rather than saying it in a caption.

### What to explicitly avoid, backed by their own numbers

- **Don't post the value proposition as a value proposition.** Every time MDST states their
  pitch directly, it's their worst-performing content, on both platforms, independently.
  Show the outcome (a great outfit, a rejected item, a happy reader) instead of the claim.
- **Don't spread thin across five channels.** MDST is proof this fails: Pinterest, Reddit
  and Substack are all live and all nearly empty because attention went to Instagram/TikTok
  instead. Pick 2–3 channels sustainable at real cadence over 5 nominal ones.
- **Don't lean on AI/tech claims in marketing copy.** It's contested ground where they
  outspend you, it's currently unvalidated even for them (opacity meter has no published
  methodology), and it's not what their own audience responds to anyway.
- **Don't compete on catalogue size.** Their number is inflated by Temu/AliExpress/boohoo —
  a bigger number is not a better number for a curated positioning, and repeating "we have
  fewer but better" turns their strength into your strength.

### Sequencing (ties to "The 3 moves" in the full competitive report)

1. Start the UGC/reader-feature format and the curation-transparency posts — cheapest to
   start, directly targets the proven engagement pattern, and reinforces the category-wide
   AI-leak differentiator.
2. Stand up Pinterest properly, with the occasion-based board taxonomy — MDST already
   proved the format works and left it uncontested.
3. Build the editorial/long-form cadence (this is the slowest to compound, so start the
   habit early) — this is the SEO/GEO surface and the "we can sustain what they can't"
   proof point.

## 3. Reusable prompt — re-run this research later

Paste this into a fresh session (or hand it to a research agent) to refresh this document
as MDST's (or any competitor's) content evolves. It's self-contained — it doesn't assume
the researcher has this conversation's context.

```
I run The Modesty House, a curated European modest-fashion discovery site (no AI search,
human-curated catalogue, affiliate links out to brands). I want to update my marketing
strategy based on what's currently working for my competitors' organic content.

Competitors to check (add/remove as the list changes):
- MDST Market — Instagram @mdst_market, TikTok @the.mdst, Pinterest mdstmarket,
  Substack mdstmarket.substack.com, subreddit r/modestshopping
- Slate — slatefashion.app (check if still live; it was a dead parked domain as of
  2026-08-13)
- Kauna — kauna.ai (the one to watch — same free+affiliate model as mine)
- [add any new competitors found]

For each competitor, on each platform they're actually active on:
1. Pull their most recent 10-15 posts. Note format (photo/carousel/reel/video), caption,
   and every engagement metric you can get without logging in (likes, comments, views,
   follower count at time of check).
2. Identify their best-performing and worst-performing posts by engagement rate, not raw
   count (normalize against follower count).
3. For the best performers specifically: what is it ABOUT the post that likely worked —
   format, subject (product vs person vs UGC vs meme vs seasonal), tone, timing? Be
   specific, not generic ("engagement" is not an answer — "a founder talking to camera"
   is).
4. For the worst performers: same analysis, what likely failed.
5. Note anything that changed since the last check: follower growth or decline, new
   channels started or abandoned, format shifts, changes in posting cadence.

Then, for each clear pattern found (e.g. "founder-facing content outperforms brand-voice
content by Nx"):
- Say plainly whether it's something I should adopt, and in what form given I'm a curated
  editorial site, not an AI-search app — don't just say "do this too," translate it to my
  format.
- Say whether it's something I'm already doing, based on [link to or paste current content
  calendar / recent posts if available].
- Flag anything that's changed enough to contradict my existing strategy in
  docs/marketing-strategy-vs-mdst.md, so I know what to revise.

Be brutally honest, cite the actual post/metric for every claim, and write "UNVERIFIED" for
anything you can't access directly (login walls, blocked APIs) rather than guessing. Do not
invent engagement numbers you couldn't retrieve. End with: what's changed since the last
version of this research, and the single highest-leverage content change I should make this
month.
```
